Up to this point, you have learned how systems work. Now you have to build one, and that changes everything. Because building forces decisions—not abstract ones, but concrete ones. What problem are you solving? Who is it for? What does success look like? And this is where most projects fail. Not in the model. In the framing.

If the problem is vague, the system will be vague. If the scope is unclear, the system will drift. So you start with clarity. Who is the user? What is the pain point? What is in scope and what is not? Now you define success, and not accuracy alone. What outcome improves? What task becomes easier? What changes for the user? Because if you cannot measure that, you cannot evaluate the system.

Now you move to data. Where does it come from? How do you access it? What constraints exist—privacy, availability, quality? Most projects break here. Not because models are hard, but because data is messy.

Now you outline the method. Start simple with a baseline, something you can build quickly. Then you improve. Because complexity without a baseline hides problems. Now you sketch the system: data, model, API, interface, and how they connect. Seeing the system early reveals issues you would miss later. Now you identify risk. What could go wrong? What assumptions are you making? And how do you test them quickly? Because the fastest way to fail is to ignore uncertainty.

Now you plan. Milestones. Ownership. What happens this week and what happens next. Because progress requires structure.

This is not about building the perfect system. It is about building a system that works within time, within constraints, and with clarity. And that mindset is what makes projects succeed. Now we move to something deeper: people.
