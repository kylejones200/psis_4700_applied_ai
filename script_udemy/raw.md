Yes. The fix is to stop treating each slide as a caption and start treating it as a teaching unit.

A class script needs four layers on every slide.

The first layer names the point.

The second layer explains what it means.

The third layer gives an example, story, or case.

The fourth layer tells the audience what to notice and what comes next.

That is why short AI scripts fail. They restate the slide. They do not teach from it.

Use this structure for each slide.

Slide title.

Opening line. State the point in plain language.

Expansion. Explain the idea in full sentences. Define terms. Add context. Say why this matters.

Example. Give a real scenario, mini case, comparison, or worked example.

Transition. Tie the slide to the next idea.

That pattern turns a 20 second script into a 90 second or 2 minute script.

Slide: How Algorithms See the Market

High-frequency trading firms watch for that first signal. The moment they detect buying activity on one exchange, they infer what is about to happen next.

They know that same order will soon hit other exchanges.

Let’s start with representation.

At its core, modern AI takes things we understand, like words or images, and turns them into numbers. That might sound abstract, but it is the key to everything that follows.

Take text as an example. When you and I read a sentence, we think in terms of meaning. We understand tone, intent, and relationships between words. A model cannot do that directly. It has to convert the sentence into a numerical form.

That numerical form is called a vector.

You can think of a vector as a list of numbers that captures meaning in a compressed way. Similar ideas produce similar vectors. Different ideas produce vectors that are farther apart.

This leads to a powerful concept.

Meaning becomes geometry.

Instead of asking whether two sentences are the same, the model asks how close their vectors are in space. That closeness can be measured using something like cosine similarity, which is a way of measuring the angle between two vectors.

If the angle is small, the meanings are similar. If the angle is large, the meanings are different.

This is how systems can find relevant documents, match queries to answers, or group similar items together. They are not reasoning in the human sense. They are measuring distance in a mathematical space.

And once you see that, a lot of modern AI starts to make sense.

Search becomes a nearest neighbor problem. Recommendations become a similarity problem. Even language generation builds on these representations.

A simple way to think about this is through probability.

Imagine a very basic system that predicts the next word in a sentence. It does not understand the sentence the way a human does. Instead, it looks at patterns it has seen before and assigns probabilities to possible next words.

This idea connects to something called a Markov process.

In a Markov process, the next state depends only on the current state, not the full history. That is a simplification, but it is useful for intuition.

For example, if the weather is sunny today, there is a certain probability it will be sunny or rainy tomorrow. The system does not need to know the entire history of weather. It only needs the current state and the transition probabilities.

Language models work in a similar way, but at a much larger scale. They look at the current context and predict what is most likely to come next. Over many steps, those predictions form sentences, paragraphs, and entire conversations.

So when you interact with a system like a chatbot, you are not talking to something that "knows" facts in a traditional sense. You are interacting with a system that is very good at predicting plausible sequences based on patterns it has learned.

That distinction matters.

It explains both the power and the limitations of these systems.

They can produce fluent, coherent output because they are strong at pattern prediction. But they can also produce confident errors because they are optimizing for plausibility, not truth.

It is a practical way to make models more accurate and more grounded in real data.
