When people hear the word "literacy," they often think of reading and writing. But literacy in any domain means something deeper: it means you can understand what is happening, you can ask the right questions, and you can make reasonable judgments without needing to be an expert. That is what we are aiming for here.

AI literacy does not mean you can build a large model from scratch. It means you understand how these systems represent information, how they make predictions, and how they align with human goals. If you can do those three things, you can work with AI effectively in almost any setting. So I want to organize this around three ideas: AI represents information, AI predicts outcomes, and AI must align with people. If you keep those three ideas in mind, everything else in this course will make more sense.

Let's start with representation. At its core, modern AI takes things we understand, like words or images, and turns them into numbers. That might sound abstract, but it is the key to everything that follows. Take text as an example. When you and I read a sentence, we think in terms of meaning. We understand tone, intent, and relationships between words. A model cannot do that directly; it has to convert the sentence into a numerical form. That numerical form is called a vector. You can think of a vector as a list of numbers that captures meaning in a compressed way. Similar ideas produce similar vectors; different ideas produce vectors that are farther apart.

This leads to a powerful concept: meaning becomes geometry. Instead of asking whether two sentences are the same, the model asks how close their vectors are in space. That closeness can be measured using something like cosine similarity, which is a way of measuring the angle between two vectors. If the angle is small, the meanings are similar; if the angle is large, the meanings are different. This is how systems can find relevant documents, match queries to answers, or group similar items together. They are not reasoning in the human sense; they are measuring distance in a mathematical space. And once you see that, a lot of modern AI starts to make sense. Search becomes a nearest neighbor problem, recommendations become a similarity problem, and even language generation builds on these representations.

Now let's move to prediction. Once information is represented numerically, the next step is to predict what comes next or what belongs together. A simple way to think about this is through probability. Imagine a very basic system that predicts the next word in a sentence. It does not understand the sentence the way a human does; instead, it looks at patterns it has seen before and assigns probabilities to possible next words.

This idea connects to something called a Markov process. In a Markov process, the next state depends only on the current state, not the full history. That is a simplification, but it is useful for intuition. For example, if the weather is sunny today, there is a certain probability it will be sunny or rainy tomorrow. The system does not need to know the entire history of weather; it only needs the current state and the transition probabilities. Language models work in a similar way, but at a much larger scale. They look at the current context and predict what is most likely to come next. Over many steps, those predictions form sentences, paragraphs, and entire conversations. So when you interact with a system like a chatbot, you are not talking to something that "knows" facts in a traditional sense. You are interacting with a system that is very good at predicting plausible sequences based on patterns it has learned. That distinction matters. It explains both the power and the limitations of these systems. They can produce fluent, coherent output because they are strong at pattern prediction, but they can also produce confident errors because they are optimizing for plausibility, not truth.

Now let's connect representation and prediction to real systems. Consider a retrieval system. You have a collection of documents. Each document is converted into a vector. When a user asks a question, that question is also converted into a vector. The system then finds the documents whose vectors are closest to the question. That is the retrieval step. Then a model uses those documents as context to generate an answer. That combination is often called retrieval-augmented generation. It is a practical way to make models more accurate and more grounded in real data. And it shows how these abstract ideas turn into working systems.

Now we move to the third idea, which is alignment. Representation and prediction are not enough on their own. A system can represent information well and make accurate predictions, but still behave in ways that are not useful or not acceptable. So we need a way to guide behavior. This is where alignment comes in. Alignment means shaping the model so that its outputs match human expectations and values. One common approach is reinforcement learning from human feedback. In simple terms, humans review model outputs and indicate which ones are better; the system then learns to prefer those outputs. You can think of this as adding a reward signal. The model still predicts, but now it is also optimizing for what humans consider good responses. This introduces a new layer of complexity: the system is no longer only learning from data, it is learning from preferences. And that raises important questions: whose preferences matter, how do we avoid bias, and how do we ensure safety. These are not purely technical questions; they are design questions, and they become more important as systems scale.

Now let's talk about where these systems run. Some computation happens on your device; some happens in the cloud. There are tradeoffs. Running models locally can improve privacy and reduce latency, but it requires more hardware. Running models in the cloud allows access to larger models, but introduces cost and potential data exposure. So even infrastructure decisions become part of AI literacy. You need to understand where your data goes and what tradeoffs you are making.

Finally, we have to address ethics and impact. AI systems learn from data. That data reflects history, and history includes bias. So models can inherit and amplify those patterns. If a dataset contains unequal outcomes across groups, a model trained on that data may reproduce those inequalities. This is why alignment matters. It is not enough to build a system that works; it has to work in a way that is fair, transparent, and accountable. And this is where AI literacy becomes practical. You do not need to solve these problems at a research level, but you do need to recognize them. You need to ask questions like who benefits from this system, who might be harmed, and what assumptions are built into the data.

So let me bring this all together. AI represents information as vectors. AI predicts outcomes using probabilities. AI must be aligned with human values. If you understand those three ideas, you can look at almost any AI system and make sense of it. You can see what it is doing, you can see where it might fail, and you can decide how to use it responsibly. That is what literacy looks like in this space: not mastery of every technique, but clarity about how the system works and where its limits are.
