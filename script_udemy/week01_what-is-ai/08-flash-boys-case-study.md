Let me start with a story that sounds almost absurd. A company spends about $300 million to build a fiber optic cable between Chicago and New Jersey. The goal is simple: they want to move data four milliseconds faster. Four milliseconds—that is less time than it takes you to blink. Now pause for a second and ask yourself a basic question: why would anyone spend that kind of money for something that small?

The answer is that in modern financial markets, speed is not a feature; speed is the product. By around 2009, trading firms no longer compete only on insight. They compete on who sees information first. If you can act even a few milliseconds faster than someone else, you can capture trades before they do. That advantage repeats thousands or millions of times per day. Each tiny edge compounds into real money. So what looks ridiculous from the outside starts to make sense inside the system. If one millisecond can be worth millions over time, then spending hundreds of millions to gain a few milliseconds becomes rational. That is the world we are stepping into: a world where machines operate at a speed no human can match, and where the structure of the system rewards whoever moves first.

Now let's look at how this speed advantage actually works in practice. When a large investor places an order to buy a stock, that order does not hit every exchange at the exact same time. It arrives at one exchange first, then reaches others a few microseconds later. That tiny delay creates a signal. High-frequency trading firms watch for that first signal. The moment they detect buying activity on one exchange, they infer what is about to happen next—they know that same order will soon hit other exchanges. So their algorithms act immediately. They rush ahead to those other exchanges and buy the stock before the original order arrives. Then, when the slower order shows up, they sell the stock back at a slightly higher price. That difference per share is tiny, often just a fraction of a cent. But this happens at massive scale—millions or billions of shares. So a tiny margin becomes a large profit. I want you to think of this as a race you never see. No alarms go off; no laws get broken. The system works exactly as designed. But the design creates an advantage for those who are faster. And that idea should sound familiar, because it mirrors many AI systems today. The structure of the system determines who wins.

Now we move to one of the most dramatic events in modern financial history. On May 6, 2010, around 2:32 in the afternoon, the market begins to fall. Within minutes, the Dow Jones drops almost 1,000 points. That is about a 9 percent drop in a very short window. Roughly $1 trillion in market value disappears. Then, almost as quickly, it comes back. During that chaos, something strange happens. Stocks begin to trade at absurd prices. Accenture, a large global company, briefly trades for one cent per share. Think about that: a company worth billions trades for one penny. That tells you something deeper is happening. This is not normal market movement; this is a system behaving in a way that no human would design intentionally. And here is the key point: this event unfolds faster than humans can react. By the time a trader sees what is happening, the damage is already done. This is a machine-speed event.

What actually caused this? The trigger was not a hacker; it was not a broken system. It was a normal trading strategy interacting with a fast system. A large mutual fund decides to sell a huge position in E-Mini S&P 500 futures. They use an algorithm to do it. The algorithm follows a simple rule: keep selling as long as there is enough volume in the market. At first, high-frequency traders buy those contracts. But they do not hold them; they quickly sell them again. Then other traders do the same thing. This creates what people call a hot potato effect. The same contracts bounce rapidly between participants, with no one willing to hold risk. Each cycle pushes prices lower. At the same time, liquidity disappears. Buyers pull back, spreads widen, and the market becomes thin. So a normal large sale turns into a cascade. Nothing in the code is broken. But the interaction between the strategy and the market structure creates a feedback loop. And once that loop starts, speed amplifies it. That is the key idea: automation does not create risk by itself; it amplifies whatever structure and incentives already exist.

At this point, we need to zoom out and look at the structure of the market itself. Modern markets do not operate in one place; orders get routed across many exchanges. This structure comes from regulation; it aims to create competition and better prices. But it also creates complexity. High-frequency traders exploit tiny differences in price across these venues. That works well in stable conditions. But in stress, something changes: those same traders pull back. They stop providing liquidity. So instead of a smooth market, you get a vacuum. Prices do not drift down; they fall off a cliff. And here is the critical part: humans cannot respond at this speed. By the time a person sees the drop, algorithms have already acted thousands of times. So control shifts from human judgment to machine behavior. That shift is what makes this story relevant to AI. We now build systems that act faster than we can supervise. And that raises a hard question: what happens when those systems face stress?

Now we introduce a person who noticed something strange. Brad Katsuyama works as a trader at RBC in New York. He tries to buy stocks, but every time he clicks buy, the price moves away before his order fills. At first, this feels like bad luck. But it keeps happening. So he starts investigating. His team runs tests, changes routing patterns, and measures timing. And they discover something important: fast traders can see his orders early and move ahead of him. They are not predicting the future; they are reacting faster than he can. That realization changes his view of the market. He concludes that the structure allows certain participants to skim value from others. This is not about illegal behavior; it is about system design. And this is another theme that carries into AI: when outcomes feel unfair, the cause is often not intent. The cause is structure.

Katsuyama decides to act. He helps build a new exchange called IEX. The core idea is simple: slow everything down. IEX introduces a 350 microsecond delay for every order. That sounds tiny, but in this world it is huge. That delay removes the advantage of being physically closer to the exchange. Now speed alone cannot win the race; everyone operates on a more level playing field. This is a design choice. Instead of rewarding speed, the system rewards fairness. And this is the broader lesson: if you change the rules, you change the behavior of the algorithms. The code does not change; the incentives do.

Let's step back and connect this story to the bigger idea. Algorithms do not have intent; they follow incentives. If you reward speed, they optimize for speed. If you reward volume, they optimize for volume. If you reward profit per microsecond, they optimize for that. The market structure defines what the algorithms do. During stable times, that can look efficient. During stress, it can look fragile. The Flash Boys story shows something important: a system can feel unfair without anyone cheating. Design choices create outcomes. And that applies directly to AI. If we reward systems for engagement, they will chase engagement. If we reward them for accuracy alone, they may ignore fairness. If we reward speed, we may get instability. So the real question is not what the model does. The real question is what we reward. That is the question you should carry forward: what incentives are we building into the systems we design?
