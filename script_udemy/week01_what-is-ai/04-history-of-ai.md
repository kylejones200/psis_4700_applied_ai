When people talk about artificial intelligence today, it can feel like it appeared suddenly. Chatbots, image generators, systems that write code—it all feels recent. But the ideas behind AI go back decades.

The formal beginning usually traces to 1956, at a meeting held at Dartmouth College. A small group of researchers gathered with a bold idea: they believed machines could simulate aspects of human intelligence, not in a vague sense but in a concrete way. They thought machines could reason, solve problems, and manipulate symbols. That moment matters because it turned AI into a field. Before that, it was speculation; after that, it became a research program. And it is important to understand how ambitious that idea was. At the time, computers were primitive, memory was limited, and processing power was minimal. Yet the belief was that intelligence could be formalized and reproduced.

Early progress followed that vision. Researchers built systems that could prove theorems and solve logical problems. These systems worked by encoding rules: if this condition holds, then apply this action. Intelligence, in this view, came from carefully designed logic. That approach led to what became known as expert systems in the 1980s. Expert systems tried to capture human expertise in a set of rules. You would interview experts, extract their knowledge, and encode it into the system. These systems saw real use in fields like medicine, finance, and engineering. And for a time, they worked well.

But they had limits. The world is messy. Rules multiply quickly, and edge cases appear everywhere. Maintaining these systems became difficult. Each new rule could conflict with existing ones, and the systems became fragile. That tension between promise and limitation is a recurring theme in AI history.

Then we move to a moment that changed public perception. In 1997, IBM's Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov, the world chess champion. Chess had long been seen as a test of intelligence—it requires strategy, foresight, and planning. So when a machine won, it felt significant. But it is important to understand how Deep Blue worked. It did not "think" like a human; it searched through many possible moves very quickly and evaluated positions using heuristics. It combined speed with structured evaluation. So the lesson was not that machines had become human-like. The lesson was that machines could outperform humans in specific domains when computation and design aligned. That distinction matters.

The next major moment comes in 2011 with IBM Watson on Jeopardy. This looks very different from chess. Jeopardy involves language, puns, ambiguity, and indirect clues. Humans rely on context and interpretation. Watson approached this problem by parsing questions, retrieving evidence, and ranking possible answers. This marked a shift: AI was moving from structured problems to messy, human-centered ones. It was not perfect, but it showed that machines could handle more than rigid logic.

Then we arrive at a breakthrough that surprised even experts. In 2016, AlphaGo defeated one of the best Go players in the world. Go is much more complex than chess; the number of possible positions is enormous. For years, experts believed it would take decades longer for machines to compete at that level. AlphaGo changed that. It combined neural networks with reinforcement learning. Instead of relying only on hand-coded rules, it learned patterns from data and from playing against itself. There is a famous moment in that match: move 37. The move looked strange to human experts and broke convention, but it turned out to be brilliant. That moment symbolizes something important: the machine was not following human intuition; it developed its own. That signals a shift from encoding knowledge to learning patterns.

Now we move beyond games into science. Around 2020, AlphaFold made a major advance in predicting protein structures. Protein folding had been a difficult problem for decades. Understanding how proteins fold is critical for biology and medicine; it affects how we understand disease and how we design drugs. AlphaFold showed that AI could produce results that matter outside of games and benchmarks. It contributed to real scientific progress. This is another turning point: AI was no longer only about competition; it became a tool for discovery.

Then we reach the transition into generative AI. Earlier systems focused on prediction—classify this image, choose the next move, rank possible answers. Generative models do something different: they create. They generate text, images, audio, and code. This changes how people interact with AI. Instead of asking for a prediction, you ask for a response. You have a conversation. You iterate. That shift makes AI more visible; it moves from the background into everyday experience.

And then we reach the most recent phase: foundation models. Starting around 2022, systems like ChatGPT, GPT-3, Claude, Gemini, and others made these capabilities widely accessible. What stands out here is the pace. Earlier milestones were spaced over decades; now improvements arrive within years, sometimes months. That acceleration comes from a combination of factors: more data, more compute, better algorithms, and a feedback loop between research and industry. This creates a very different environment. Progress feels continuous and it feels fast.

So when you look at the present moment, it can seem like a sudden leap. But it is better to see it as a long arc: from symbolic reasoning to statistical learning to generative systems. Each phase builds on the previous one, and each phase changes what we think intelligence looks like. So when you see a modern AI system, you are not looking at something that appeared overnight. You are looking at the result of decades of ideas, experiments, failures, and breakthroughs. And that perspective matters, because it reminds you that the field will continue to change. New approaches will emerge, new limitations will appear, and new questions will follow. So the goal is not to memorize each milestone. The goal is to understand the trajectory: from rules to learning, from narrow tasks to broader capabilities, from prediction to creation. That is the story of AI so far, and we are still early in that story.
