Most of our examples have involved text or structured data. Now we move into images, and this is where something important becomes clear. AI does not see the world the way you do. When you look at an image, you see objects. You see context. You recognize meaning almost instantly—a child, a dog, a road, a face. You do not think about pixels. A computer has no access to that experience. It starts with numbers.

Every image is converted into a grid. Each position in that grid represents a pixel. Each pixel has values that describe brightness or color. For a black and white image, that value might be simple: zero for black, one for white. For a color image, each pixel carries three values: red, green, and blue. Together, those numbers describe the full image. So when a model receives an image, it does not see a picture. It sees a matrix. And that matrix is the starting point for everything that follows.

Imagine a very small image. Three by three pixels. Each pixel is either zero or one. To you, that might look like a simple shape. To the model, it is a grid of numbers—something like a pattern of zeros and ones arranged in space. That is the entire input. From that point on, everything the model does is mathematical. It looks for patterns in those numbers. It does not know what a cat is. It does not know what a face is. It learns which patterns tend to appear together and which patterns lead to certain labels.

The first layer of a vision model looks for very simple patterns: changes in intensity, edges, lines. These are local features. They do not represent objects; they represent basic structure. The next layer builds on those features. It combines edges into shapes and detects corners, textures, and repeated patterns. As you move deeper into the network, the patterns become more abstract. Shapes combine into parts. Parts combine into objects. And eventually, the model can recognize something like a dog or a car. But it is important to understand what recognition means here. The model has not formed a concept in the human sense. It has learned that certain combinations of pixel patterns tend to correspond to certain labels. That is enough to make accurate predictions.

Once the model reaches that point, it can perform different types of tasks. In classification, the goal is simple: given an image, assign a label. This is a dog. This is a bird. The model compares the patterns it sees to patterns it has learned. It produces a probability. A high probability means the pattern matches strongly. In object detection, instead of labeling the entire image, the model identifies where objects are. It draws boxes around them. Those boxes come from scanning different regions of the image and evaluating each one. The output is not only what is present, but where it is located. In tracking, for video, objects move. The model identifies the same object across frames and follows it over time. To do that, it compares patterns from one frame to the next, estimates motion, and maintains identity. In segmentation, instead of drawing a box, the model labels each pixel. Every pixel belongs to a category—foreground or background, object or not object. This creates a detailed map of the image. And that level of precision enables applications like medical imaging, where identifying exact boundaries matters.

It is easy to assume that vision is limited to what humans can see. But that is not true. Computers are not constrained by the human eye. They can process data from different parts of the spectrum: infrared, ultraviolet, LIDAR. Each of these produces a different kind of input. Not a picture in the traditional sense, but still a matrix of values. Infrared captures heat. So instead of color, you see temperature gradients. That allows systems to detect people in low light or through smoke. LIDAR measures distance. It sends out pulses and records how long they take to return. The result is a three-dimensional map—a point cloud that describes shape and space. In each case, the underlying structure is the same. Data becomes numbers, numbers become patterns, patterns become predictions. So whether the input is a photograph, a thermal image, or a 3D scan, the model operates in the same way.

Vision models do not understand context the way humans do. They rely on patterns. If those patterns change, the model can fail. A slight distortion, a different lighting condition, an unusual angle—these changes can break the patterns the model has learned. This is why vision systems can be both powerful and fragile. They perform well when the input matches the training data. They struggle when it does not. And that brings us back to a familiar theme. The model reflects the data. If the data is diverse and representative, the model becomes more robust. If it is narrow, the model becomes brittle.

In manufacturing, vision systems detect defects. They scan products and identify irregularities. In transportation, they detect lanes, obstacles, and signs. In healthcare, they analyze medical images to support diagnosis. In agriculture, they monitor crops and detect disease. Each application uses the same underlying process. But the constraints differ. Accuracy requirements change. Environmental conditions change. Risk tolerance changes. So the design must adapt.

A computer does not see images. It processes numbers. Meaning emerges from patterns in those numbers. Once you understand that, you understand both the strength and the limitation of computer vision. It can detect patterns at scale. But it depends entirely on the data it has seen. And that is the lens you should carry forward as we move into how AI creates value in business.
