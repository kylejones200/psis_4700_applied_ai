Time series data looks simple at first glance—a sequence of numbers over time: prices, demand, temperature, sensor readings. The order is what makes it different. In most data problems, rows can be shuffled without changing meaning, but in time series, order is the meaning. Break the order and the problem no longer makes sense. That single constraint shapes everything that follows.

Start with the basic components. A time series often contains three elements: a trend, which is the long-term direction; seasonality, or repeating patterns; and noise, or random variation. These are not always cleanly separated, but they guide how you think. If you ignore them, your model will struggle. Splitting the data requires care. You cannot randomly split time series data: training must use the past, and testing must use the future. Otherwise you leak information and give the model knowledge it would not have in reality. So evaluation must respect time—this is not optional.

Before building complex models, you establish baselines. Repeat the last value, repeat the last seasonal value, or use a moving average. These are simple, but they often perform better than expected. If your advanced model cannot beat them, it has no value. This is a recurring theme across all of machine learning. Error measurement reflects the nature of the data: mean absolute error captures average deviation, root mean squared error penalizes large mistakes more heavily, and percentage errors normalize across scale. Each metric tells a different story, so choose based on what matters in the real world.

Classical models still matter. ARIMA captures autoregressive patterns and moving averages. SARIMA extends this with seasonality, and exponential smoothing emphasizes recent observations. These models are interpretable, they encode assumptions about the data, and they remain strong baselines even as newer methods emerge. Feature engineering expands what the model sees: lag features bring past values into the present, rolling statistics capture local trends, and calendar features encode known patterns such as day of week or holidays. These features inject structure into the model; without them, the model must infer everything from raw sequences.

A key decision appears in how models are trained. Local models handle each series separately, while global models learn across many series. Global models can share patterns and generalize better when data is sparse, and this becomes important in large systems. Modern approaches adapt ideas from language models: transformers process sequences, and time series can be treated as sequences of values. Models like Chronos or TinyTimeMixer apply this structure—they learn patterns across many series, capture complex dependencies, and can outperform classical methods when data supports them. But they also introduce cost and complexity.

Evaluation must match deployment. Rolling-origin evaluation simulates real use: train on past data, predict the next step, move forward, and repeat. This produces a realistic estimate of performance. Single splits do not capture this dynamic. Certain patterns require special handling: intermittent demand includes many zeros, and standard models fail here, so methods like Croston's approach handle this structure. Missing data introduces gaps—you may impute values or model the gaps directly—and each choice affects results.

Multivariate series extend the problem. External variables influence outcomes: weather affects energy demand, promotions affect sales. Including these variables improves forecasts when relationships are real, but not all features help—some introduce noise. Uncertainty matters. Point predictions give a single value, but probabilistic forecasts provide ranges—intervals or quantiles. Decision-makers rarely need a single number; they need a range of possible outcomes. Calibration ensures these ranges are meaningful: if you predict a 90 percent interval, it should contain the true value about 90 percent of the time.

Real systems face change. Regime shifts alter behavior, consumer patterns change, markets shift. Models trained on past data assume stability, and when that assumption breaks, performance drops. Change point detection helps identify these shifts, and models must adapt. Anomaly detection builds on forecasting: you predict expected values, compare actual values, and large deviations signal anomalies. Context matters—high demand in December may be normal, but the same value in January may not be.

Deployment introduces operational decisions. How often do you update forecasts? Daily, weekly, monthly—each cadence has tradeoffs. More frequent updates respond faster; less frequent updates reduce cost. Backtesting validates updates before deployment, and this prevents silent failure. Visualization supports understanding: decomposition separates trend, seasonality, and noise, and forecast versus actual plots reveal where models succeed or fail. These are not cosmetic; they guide decisions.

Advanced topics extend further. Hierarchical forecasting ensures consistency across levels—product-level forecasts must align with category totals. Cross-series learning allows related series to share information, and cold start problems require initialization from similar series. Production systems require reliability: forecasts must arrive on time, fallback methods must exist, and if the primary model fails, a baseline can take over. Service level agreements define expectations.

Step back and the structure becomes clear. Time series adds one constraint: time. Everything must respect it—data splits, evaluation, features, models. Once you honor that constraint, the problem becomes manageable. Ignore it, and results become misleading. Reflection matters here: what horizon matters in your domain—tomorrow, next week, next year? What metric reflects success—accuracy, cost, risk? Those answers shape the system more than the model choice. That is Week 6.
