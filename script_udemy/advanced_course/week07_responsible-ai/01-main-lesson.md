At this point, the focus shifts—not what the system can do, but what it should do. Because capability without constraint creates risk, and as systems become more powerful, that risk grows. Responsible AI begins with impact: AI systems influence decisions, those decisions affect people, and the stakes are real in domains like hiring, lending, healthcare, and safety. So responsibility is not an add-on; it is part of the system.

The core principles provide a foundation. Fairness ensures outcomes do not systematically disadvantage groups. Accountability ensures someone is responsible for decisions. Transparency allows people to understand what the system does. Privacy protects sensitive information, and safety prevents harm. These are not abstract ideas; they translate into design choices. Bias enters the system at many points: in the data, where historical patterns may reflect past discrimination; in the labels, where human annotators bring their own assumptions; in the model, where patterns can be amplified; and in deployment, where context may change how outputs are used. Each stage requires attention, and ignoring one is enough to introduce risk.

Fairness is not a single definition. Different metrics capture different ideas: demographic parity focuses on equal outcomes, equalized odds focuses on equal error rates, and calibration ensures probabilities mean the same across groups. These definitions can conflict, and you cannot satisfy all at once, so you must choose—and that choice reflects values. Interventions exist at multiple points. Before training, you adjust the data by reweighting examples or balancing classes. During training, you constrain the model by forcing fairness conditions. After training, you adjust outputs by changing thresholds across groups. Each method has tradeoffs, and none is perfect.

Explainability helps build understanding. Global explanations describe how the model behaves overall, while local explanations describe a specific prediction. Model-agnostic methods like LIME approximate behavior, and SHAP assigns contributions based on game theory. These tools provide insight, but they do not guarantee correctness—they approximate, so interpretation requires care. Interpretability starts earlier: simple models are easier to explain, while complex models require post-hoc methods. The choice depends on the problem, and high-stakes decisions often favor interpretability.

Privacy introduces another dimension. Data should be minimized, collected only when needed, stored securely, and anonymized where possible. Advanced techniques go further: differential privacy adds noise to protect individuals, and federated learning trains models without centralizing data. These methods balance utility and protection. Human oversight remains essential. High-risk decisions require review, systems must allow escalation, and people must be able to intervene. Automation does not remove responsibility; it shifts it.

Documentation supports transparency. Model cards describe performance, limitations, and intended use. Datasheets describe data sources and collection methods. System cards describe how components interact. These documents create shared understanding and support accountability. Monitoring continues after deployment: data drift changes input distributions, performance may vary across groups, safety issues may emerge, and feedback from users provides signals. The system must adapt. Red teaming tests the system deliberately through adversarial inputs, edge cases, and prompt injections. The goal is to find failure modes before users do, and preparation reduces impact.

Compliance introduces external constraints. Frameworks such as NIST or OECD define expectations, and regulations vary by sector. Understanding these constraints is part of system design. Fairness often conflicts with accuracy—improving one may reduce the other—so these tradeoffs must be made explicit. You define objectives, you measure outcomes, and you choose deliberately. Causal thinking adds depth: counterfactual fairness asks what would happen if a sensitive attribute changed, and structural models capture relationships. These approaches move beyond correlation and address underlying mechanisms.

Human factors remain central. Over-reliance creates risk when users trust outputs without question, so design must encourage engagement rather than blind acceptance. Redress mechanisms provide recourse: users must be able to challenge decisions, correct errors, and escalate concerns. This reinforces trust. Incident management prepares for failure through detection, containment, communication, and postmortem. Systems must respond when things go wrong—not if. Audits evaluate systems externally and internally: they test performance across groups, assess compliance, and identify gaps. Regular audits maintain accountability. The regulatory landscape continues to evolve; policies change, standards develop, and organizations must adapt. Static systems fall behind.

Step back and the structure becomes clear. Responsible AI is not a single step—it spans the lifecycle: design, data, modeling, deployment, and monitoring. Each stage carries responsibility. The final reflection matters: which fairness definition fits your domain, what risks matter most, and what tradeoffs are acceptable. Because those answers shape the system more than any model choice. That is Week 7.
