# Module 1: Foundations of Applied AI

## Module Overview

This module introduces applied artificial intelligence as it appears in real organizations. Students learn how applied AI differs from research, hype, and experimentation. The module establishes shared language and mental models used throughout the course. Students practice framing a real workplace problem as a candidate for applied AI.

## Module Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, students can:

1. Distinguish applied AI from research and hype driven AI.
2. Describe common workplace AI use cases and failure modes.
3. Frame a business problem as a candidate for applied AI.

## Lesson 1: What Applied AI Is and Is Not

### Lesson Objective
This lesson supports the objective of distinguishing applied AI from research and hype driven AI.

### Lesson Overview
Applied AI solves bounded problems inside existing workflows. It values reliability, cost control, and clarity over novelty. This lesson establishes what counts as applied AI and what does not.

### Core Content
Applied AI focuses on outcomes, not models. Organizations use AI to reduce effort, increase consistency, or improve decision quality. These systems operate under constraints that include limited data, fixed budgets, regulatory rules, and human oversight.

Research AI prioritizes novelty and performance benchmarks. It explores what is possible under ideal conditions. Applied AI prioritizes what works tomorrow inside an organization that already exists.

Hype driven AI emphasizes tools over problems. It often begins with a model and searches for a use case. Applied AI begins with a problem and selects the simplest tool that meets the need.

In practice, applied AI often uses imperfect data, off the shelf services, and conservative designs. Success depends on fit, not sophistication.

### Worked Example
An internal document search assistant uses embeddings and retrieval rather than a custom trained language model. The goal is faster access to existing knowledge, not model innovation.

### Check for Understanding
Students identify whether a described scenario represents applied AI, research AI, or hype driven AI and explain why.

## Lesson 2: Where AI Fits in Everyday Work

### Lesson Objective
This lesson supports the objective of describing common workplace AI use cases and failure modes.

### Lesson Overview
AI rarely replaces entire jobs. It supports specific tasks inside broader workflows. This lesson examines where applied AI creates value and where it commonly fails.

### Core Content
Applied AI fits best where work follows patterns. Examples include document classification, summarization, forecasting, anomaly detection, and decision support.

Successful use cases share common traits. The task repeats. The cost of error is known. Humans remain in the loop. Output integrates into existing tools.

Failure often follows predictable paths. Teams automate unclear processes. Data access breaks trust. Outputs lack explanation. Systems cost more than the work they replace.

Applied AI succeeds when teams choose narrow problems and define success early.

### Worked Example
A support team uses AI to draft response suggestions. Agents review and edit before sending. Productivity increases without removing human judgment.

### Check for Understanding
Students match AI techniques to workplace tasks and identify likely failure points.

## Lesson 3: Problem Framing for Applied AI

### Lesson Objective
This lesson supports the objective of framing a business problem as a candidate for applied AI.

### Lesson Overview
Problem framing determines success more than model choice. This lesson teaches a structured approach to deciding whether AI fits a given problem.

### Core Content
Applied AI problem framing begins with the business context. Students identify the task, the user, and the desired outcome. Constraints follow. Data availability, risk tolerance, cost limits, and deployment environment shape feasibility.

Clear success criteria matter. Faster completion, fewer errors, or improved consistency define value. Vague goals create fragile systems.

Not every problem deserves AI. Rule based solutions often perform better. Saying no protects teams and budgets.

A framed problem describes what changes if the system succeeds and what happens if it fails.

### Worked Example
A forecasting task specifies time horizon, acceptable error, decision impact, and fallback behavior. The framing guides tool selection later in the course.

### Check for Understanding
Students draft a short problem statement and identify constraints and success criteria.

## Module 1 Assignment: Applied AI Problem Framing Memo

### Assignment Overview
Students identify a real workplace problem and frame it as a candidate for applied AI. The assignment emphasizes clarity, constraints, and decision quality.

### Assignment Instructions
Students submit a one page memo. The memo describes the business context, the task, the user, and the desired outcome. It lists key constraints related to data, cost, risk, and deployment. It states clear success criteria and identifies one reason the project could fail.

### Assessment Criteria
- The memo clearly distinguishes applied AI from research or hype.
- The problem statement is specific and grounded in real work.
- Constraints are realistic and relevant.
- Success criteria are measurable and understandable.

### Alignment
This assignment assesses Module 1 objective three and Course Learning Outcome one.
